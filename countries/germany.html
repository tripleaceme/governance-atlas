Executive summary

Compiled from public sources · not legal advice

Germany applies the EU GDPR directly, supplemented by the Federal Data Protection Act (BDSG), enacted in 2017 and effective alongside the GDPR on 25 May 2018. The BDSG uses the GDPR's opening clauses extensively: it lowers the DPO-appointment threshold (mandatory once 20 or more people regularly process personal data), adds detailed rules on employee data processing (§26 BDSG), credit scoring, and video surveillance, and each of the 16 Länder has its own state data protection law for its public sector.

Supervision is uniquely decentralised. The federal BfDI oversees federal public bodies and telecommunications/postal providers, while 17 independent state authorities (Landesdatenschutzbehörden) supervise the private sector and state public bodies — so a company's lead German regulator depends on where it is established. Coordination happens through the Datenschutzkonferenz (DSK), which issues joint guidance. German authorities are prolific enforcers, with landmark fines against H&M (€35m) and others, and German courts see heavy GDPR damages litigation.

For businesses, the German specifics that matter most are the low DPO threshold, strict works-council and employee-data rules, conservative positions on consent and tracking (reinforced by the TDDDG for cookies), and the need to identify the correct state authority. Enforcement culture is formalistic and complaint-driven, and audits are common.

Key obligations

  • 01
    Appoint a DPO at the 20-person threshold — Designate a data protection officer whenever at least 20 persons are regularly engaged in automated processing of personal data — stricter than the GDPR baseline (§38 BDSG).
  • 02
    Employee data rules — Process employee data only as permitted by §26 BDSG and collective agreements; works councils have co-determination rights over monitoring systems.
  • 03
    Breach notification — Notify the competent (usually state) supervisory authority within 72 hours of a personal data breach.
  • 04
    Records and accountability — Maintain records of processing activities and demonstrate GDPR compliance; no registration with authorities is required.
  • 05
    DPIA for high-risk processing — Conduct impact assessments for high-risk processing, consulting the DSK blacklists published by German authorities.
