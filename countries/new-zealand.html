Executive summary

Compiled from public sources · not legal advice

New Zealand's Privacy Act 2020 (replacing the 1993 Act) governs how 'agencies' — nearly every public body and private organisation, including overseas businesses carrying on business in NZ — handle personal information, structured around 13 Information Privacy Principles (IPPs) covering collection, storage, access, correction, accuracy, retention, use, disclosure and unique identifiers. Distinctive features include a mandatory privacy officer for every agency (a broader requirement than the GDPR's conditional DPO), a principles-based rather than consent-based collection model, mandatory breach notification to the Privacy Commissioner and affected individuals when serious harm is likely, and IPP 12 controls on overseas disclosure. New Zealand holds an EU adequacy decision — one of the few Asia-Pacific jurisdictions to do so.

Enforcement is compliance-oriented rather than punitive: the Privacy Commissioner can investigate complaints, issue compliance notices and access directions, but criminal fines max out at NZD 10,000; substantive remedies flow through the Human Rights Review Tribunal, which can award damages for interference with privacy. The regime's teeth are reputational and remedial rather than turnover-based penalties — a frequent law-reform criticism.

The framework is actively evolving through sector codes: the Biometric Processing Privacy Code 2025, issued 21 July 2025 and in force 3 November 2025, imposes a necessity/proportionality test, mandatory privacy impact assessment, transparency notices and bias assessment (including for Māori and Pacific peoples) on agencies using biometrics, with a grace period for existing users ending 3 August 2026. Health, credit reporting and telecommunications codes similarly modify the IPPs for their sectors.

Key obligations

  • 01
    Comply with the 13 Information Privacy Principles — Collect personal information only for lawful purposes directly from the individual where practicable, be transparent about collection, keep it secure and accurate, retain it no longer than necessary, and use/disclose it only for the purpose collected or permitted exceptions.
  • 02
    Appoint a privacy officer — Every agency must have a privacy officer responsible for encouraging compliance, dealing with access requests and complaints, and liaising with the Office of the Privacy Commissioner (s 201).
  • 03
    Notify privacy breaches — Notify the Privacy Commissioner (via NotifyUs) and affected individuals as soon as practicable after any privacy breach that has caused or is likely to cause serious harm; failure to notify is an offence.
  • 04
    Control overseas disclosures (IPP 12) — Before disclosing personal information to a foreign entity, ensure comparable privacy safeguards apply — through the destination's law, contract, binding scheme or prescribed-country status — or obtain express informed consent.
  • 05
    Respond to access and correction requests — Individuals have enforceable rights to access and request correction of their information; agencies must respond within 20 working days, and the Commissioner can issue binding access directions.
