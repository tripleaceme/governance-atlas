Executive summary

Compiled from public sources · not legal advice

Denmark is governed directly by the GDPR, supplemented by the Danish Data Protection Act (databeskyttelsesloven, Act No. 502 of 23 May 2018), which replaced the earlier Act on Processing of Personal Data from 25 May 2018. The Act exercises GDPR opening clauses on matters such as CPR (civil registration) numbers, employment data and sets the digital age of consent at the GDPR minimum of 13.

Denmark's most distinctive feature is its enforcement mechanics: because Danish constitutional tradition does not allow administrative authorities to impose punitive fines, Datatilsynet cannot fine directly. Instead it reports violators to the police with a recommended fine, and Danish courts impose the penalty through criminal proceedings — GDPR Recital 151 explicitly accommodates this. Serious violations can even carry up to six months' imprisonment. This makes headline fines rarer and slower than elsewhere in the EU (the largest court-confirmed fine to date is around DKK 1 million against Arp-Hansen Hotels), though Datatilsynet is highly active through orders, bans and influential guidance — including its prominent positions on Google Analytics and cloud services.

For organisations, day-to-day compliance is standard GDPR practice — lawful basis, transparency, 72-hour breach notification, DPIAs and transfer safeguards — with Danish specifics around CPR numbers and the court-based sanction route worth knowing before any enforcement encounter.

Key obligations

  • 01
    Comply with full GDPR obligations — Lawful basis, transparency, data subject rights, records of processing, DPIAs and accountability apply directly under the GDPR as supplemented by databeskyttelsesloven.
  • 02
    Notify breaches within 72 hours — Personal data breaches posing a risk to individuals must be reported to Datatilsynet within 72 hours.
  • 03
    Handle CPR numbers restrictively — Danish civil registration (CPR) numbers may only be processed by private entities under the specific conditions in the Data Protection Act, such as legal requirement or consent.
  • 04
    Appoint a DPO where required — Mandatory for public authorities and organisations with large-scale monitoring or special-category processing as core activities.
  • 05
    Obtain parental consent under 13 — Online services relying on consent must obtain parental authorisation for children under 13, per section 6(3) of the Act.
