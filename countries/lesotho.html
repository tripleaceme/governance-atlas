Executive summary

Compiled from public sources · not legal advice

Lesotho enacted one of Southern Africa's earliest data protection statutes — the Data Protection Act, published in the Government Gazette on 22 February 2012 — making the recorded status of an in-force law correct. The Act applies to automated and manual processing, sets out familiar principles (minimality, purpose specification, security safeguards), treats children's data as sensitive personal information requiring parental consent, requires controllers to notify the Data Protection Commission before processing, and mandates breach notification 'as soon as reasonably possible' to the Commission and affected individuals. Its transfer rules are distinctive for privileging SADC member states that have transposed equivalent protections.

The fatal weakness is institutional: the Data Protection Commission the Act establishes has never been appointed. Fourteen years on there is no commissioner, no registration machinery, no guidance and no enforcement — local practitioners describe the law as having 'no teeth'. Even if constituted, the Commission's powers are weaker than modern peers; notably, it cannot impose administrative fines, leaving only limited criminal penalties.

Practically, organisations in Lesotho face a technically enforceable statute with zero supervisory activity, and reform advocates are pressing for a modernised law and an operational authority. Compliance is prudent (courts could still apply the Act, and enforcement could reach past conduct once a Commission exists), but there is no regulator to register with today.

Key obligations

  • 01
    Notify the Commission before processing — Section 25(5) requires controllers to process personal information only upon notification to the Data Protection Commission — currently impossible in practice as no Commission exists.
  • 02
    Report security compromises promptly — Controllers must notify the Commission and affected data subjects of security compromises as soon as reasonably possible after discovery.
  • 03
    Handle sensitive and children's data strictly — Children's data is sensitive personal information; processing generally requires prior parental consent.
  • 04
    Assess transfers outside SADC — Transfers to non-SADC countries require an adequate level of protection assessed against the data, purpose, destination law and security measures.
