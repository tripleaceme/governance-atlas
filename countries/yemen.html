Executive summary

Compiled from public sources · not legal advice

Yemen has no data protection law, no draft bill in meaningful progress, and no supervisory authority. Privacy is addressed only in fragments: Article 52 of the Constitution guarantees the sanctity of private correspondence and postal, telephonic and electronic communications, which may be monitored or intercepted only under a legal warrant; the Penal Code and criminal procedure rules touch on privacy of the home and correspondence; and telecommunications regulation obliges licensed operators to keep customer communications confidential.

The most relevant modern statute is Law No. 13 of 2012 on the Right of Access to Information, which is a freedom-of-information law rather than a data protection law, though it contains some limits on disclosure of personal information held by public bodies. Civil society assessments (e.g., SAM for Rights and Liberties) consistently conclude that Yemen lacks any dedicated legislation protecting or organizing users' data against misuse by public or private actors.

The ongoing armed conflict and the split between rival authorities in Sana'a and Aden make new national legislation and any enforcement infrastructure unrealistic in the near term. Organizations handling data of Yemeni individuals should apply their own group-level standards, as there is no local compliance framework, regulator or breach-notification duty to satisfy.
