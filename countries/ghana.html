Executive summary

Compiled from public sources · not legal advice

Ghana was an early African mover on data protection: the Data Protection Act, 2012 (Act 843) has been in force since October 2012 and established the Data Protection Commission (DPC), one of the continent's more active regulators. The Act applies to anyone processing personal data using equipment or a controller in Ghana, imposes eight data protection principles broadly modeled on pre-GDPR European law, and gives data subjects rights of access, correction and compensation. Its most distinctive operational feature is mandatory registration: every data controller must register with the DPC and renew every two years, and processing without registration is a criminal offence.

Enforcement is real but modest by modern standards — penalties are criminal fines (capped around GHS 60,000) and imprisonment rather than GDPR-style turnover-based administrative fines, and the Act lacks a fixed breach-notification deadline, a mandatory DPO, and a dedicated cross-border transfer regime. The DPC compensates with sustained registration and compliance campaigns, and in practice pushes larger controllers to designate data protection supervisors.

The framework is on the cusp of a major overhaul: a Data Protection Bill, 2025 would repeal and replace Act 843 with a far more stringent regime — turnover-linked penalties, detailed cross-border transfer rules with data-localization preferences (mandatory localization for children's, biometric, health and national-identity data), parental consent requirements, and rules on automated decision-making. As of mid-2026 the Bill was still being finalized for introduction in Parliament, so Act 843 remains the operative law for any near-term compliance discussion.

Key obligations

  • 01
    Register with the DPC — All data controllers must register with the Data Protection Commission before processing and renew registration every two years; unregistered processing is a criminal offence.
  • 02
    Apply the eight data protection principles — Processing must be lawful, fair, purpose-specific, adequate and not excessive, accurate, retention-limited, respectful of data subject rights, and secure.
  • 03
    Obtain consent or another lawful basis — Personal data may only be processed with the data subject's consent or another recognized justification such as contract, legal obligation, or legitimate interest.
  • 04
    Secure data and report compromises — Controllers must take reasonable technical and organizational measures and notify the Commission and affected individuals of security compromises as soon as reasonably practicable.
  • 05
    Honor data subject rights — Individuals can demand access, correction, and deletion of their data and claim compensation for damage caused by non-compliant processing.
