Executive summary

Compiled from public sources · not legal advice

Afghanistan has no data protection law of any kind, and none is in prospect. Before August 2021, partial protections existed on paper: Article 37 of the 2004 Constitution protected confidentiality of communications, and sectoral instruments (telecom regulation, a 2017 Cybercrime Law) touched on data confidentiality. Since the Taliban takeover, the 2004 Constitution has been effectively suspended and no functioning legal framework, regulator, or judicial recourse for privacy exists.

The privacy situation is not merely a legal vacuum — it is an acute human rights risk. Two decades of donor-funded, data-intensive systems left behind extensive digital identity, biometric (iris, fingerprint, photograph), payroll and HR databases that are now in Taliban hands. Human Rights Watch and Privacy International have documented how these systems can be used to identify former government employees, security personnel, and NGO workers, with potentially lethal consequences.

For any organization still operating in or handling data about Afghanistan, the practical guidance is data minimization and protection as an ethical/security imperative rather than a compliance exercise: there is no authority to register with, no breach regime, and no lawful-transfer analysis to perform — only the duty of care owed to data subjects facing real-world danger.
