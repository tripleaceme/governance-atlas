Executive summary

Compiled from public sources · not legal advice

Syria has no comprehensive data protection or privacy law, and no bill for one is known to be in progress as of August 2026. Privacy protections are fragmentary: the Constitutional Declaration of 13 March 2025, issued by the transitional government after the fall of the Assad government in December 2024, contains general rights guarantees, and older sectoral rules (telecommunications regulation, banking secrecy, and general civil/criminal provisions on correspondence and defamation) provide narrow, uneven coverage.

The closest thing to data-related legislation is the Assad-era Cybercrime Law No. 20 of 2022, which criminalises certain misuse of information systems and online content but is primarily a speech-control instrument, with vague offences such as 'undermining the prestige of the state'. Human rights organisations — including Amnesty International and the Syrian Network for Human Rights — have documented its continued use by transitional authorities against journalists and activists through 2026. In June 2026 the Ministry of Justice announced a review of the law to harmonise it with the Constitutional Declaration and international standards, and introduced some limits on its use, but it remains in force.

For any organisation handling Syrian personal data, the practical position is that there is no supervisory authority, no registration, breach-notification or transfer regime, and no data-subject rights framework. Compliance decisions should be driven by the laws of the organisation's own jurisdiction (e.g. GDPR for EU entities), sanctions considerations, and heightened ethical duties of care given the acute risks that data exposure poses to individuals in Syria's transitional context.
