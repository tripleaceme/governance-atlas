Executive summary

Compiled from public sources · not legal advice

Iran has no comprehensive data protection law in force; a draft has been in progress for years without enactment. The best-known text is the Draft Bill on Protection of Data and Privacy in Cyberspace (2018), prepared by the ICT Ministry, and successor drafts of a Personal Data Protection and Safeguarding Bill have circulated through government and parliament (Majlis) since, but none has become law as of mid-2026. No national data protection authority exists.

In the meantime, privacy protection is scattered across sectoral and general instruments: the Electronic Commerce Law (2004) contains consent-based rules for personal data in electronic transactions; the Computer/Cyber Crimes Act (2011) criminalizes publishing another person's images, recordings or personal and family secrets without consent (61 days to 6 months' imprisonment or IRR 1-10 million fines); the non-binding Charter on Citizens' Rights (2016), the Law on Publication of and Access to Data (2010), the Constitution and Sharia principles round out the picture.

For businesses, there are no registration, DPO, breach notification or cross-border transfer requirements — but data practices intersect with Iran's heavy internet controls, localization pressures via the National Information Network agenda, and criminal exposure for wrongful disclosure. Sanctions considerations dominate any practical engagement. The draft bill's timetable remains unclear, and its passage would likely still leave enforcement in the hands of state bodies rather than an independent DPA.

Key obligations

  • 01
    E-Commerce Law consent rules — In electronic transactions, personal data must be collected and processed with the data subject's consent, for clear purposes, and kept accurate — the closest Iran comes to general processing rules.
  • 02
    Do not disclose private material — The Cyber Crimes Act criminalizes publishing another person's films, pictures, sounds, or personal and family secrets without consent, with imprisonment or fines.
  • 03
    Respect constitutional/Charter privacy — The Constitution and the 2016 Charter on Citizens' Rights recognize privacy of persons, communications and correspondence, informing how courts treat data misuse.
