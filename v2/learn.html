Data privacy law, for people who are not lawyers

What the law actually asks of you, whether it applies to your organisation, the words you will hear, the people to involve — and the smallest set of steps that gets you started.

The big idea

What data privacy law is really about

Strip away the legal language and every data protection law says roughly the same thing: information about a person belongs, in an important sense, to that person. If you hold it, you are holding something borrowed. You need a good reason to have it, you must be honest about what you do with it, you must keep it safe, and you must let people see and correct what you hold.

That is the whole idea. The differences between countries are mostly about how strictly those duties are enforced, who polices them, and how large the penalty is when they are ignored.

Two things surprise people most. These laws apply to ordinary organisations, not just tech giants — a clinic with a patient list, a school with parent contacts, a shop with a mailing list. And they often apply across borders: the law that matters is usually the law where your customers are, not where your office is.

Applies to you?

Five questions that settle it

If any of these are true, at least one data protection law already covers what you do.

✓

You keep names, emails, phone numbers or addresses

For customers, staff, job applicants, donors, patients or students.

✓

You have customers, users or employees in another country

That country's law may reach you even if you have no office there.

✓

You use analytics, advertising, email marketing or a CRM

These collect and share personal data by design.

✓

You handle sensitive information

Health, biometrics, financial details, religion, political views, or anything about children.

✓

Someone else processes data for you

A payroll provider, cloud host or agency. their handling is still your responsibility.

Almost every organisation ticks a box. The useful question is not whether a law applies, but which — start with your country's brief.

Vocabulary

The words you will hear

The terms that make every country page, contract and regulator letter readable.

Personal data
Any information that identifies a living person — a name, an email, an ID number, a location, even an IP address in many jurisdictions.
Data subject
The person the data is about. Laws grant them rights; you owe them duties.
Controller
The organisation that decides why and how data is processed. Most obligations land here — usually you.
Processor
Anyone handling data on the controller's instructions — a cloud host, payroll firm or agency. They carry their own duties too.
Lawful basis
The legal reason you may process data at all: consent, a contract, a legal obligation, vital interests, public task, or legitimate interests.
Privacy notice
The public statement telling people what you collect, why, who you share it with and how to reach you. Usually the first thing a regulator checks.
Data Protection Officer
The person accountable for privacy day to day. Mandatory above certain thresholds in many countries; South Africa calls the role an Information Officer.
Supervisory authority
The regulator that enforces the law, receives breach reports and issues fines. Every country page names yours.
Personal data breach
Any incident where data is lost, stolen, altered or exposed — not only hacking. A misdirected email counts.
DPIA
A written risk assessment done before starting something high-risk, such as large-scale profiling or new surveillance technology.
Cross-border transfer
Sending personal data to another country — including simply using a cloud service hosted abroad.
Adequacy decision
A formal finding that another country protects data to an equivalent standard, letting data flow there freely. Few countries hold one.
Standard Contractual Clauses
Pre-approved contract terms binding whoever receives your data abroad to protect it properly. The main route where no adequacy decision exists.
Retention
How long you keep data. “Forever” is not a lawful answer — set a period tied to the purpose, then delete.
Your team

Who you will be talking to

Privacy work is a team sport. These are the people involved, and when to bring each one in.

1

Whoever runs your data

Your engineers, analysts or IT provider are the only people who truly know where personal data lives and what your tools quietly collect.

From day one
2

An accountable owner

One named person responsible for privacy — a DPO where the law requires it, otherwise simply whoever will answer for it.

From day one
3

Your vendors and processors

Every tool that touches customer data needs a written agreement covering what they may do with it and where it goes.

Before you sign
4

A privacy lawyer or licensed consultancy

For binding advice, cross-border contracts, filings and audits. Some countries license specific firms for this work.

At decision points
5

Your regulator

Not only an enforcer — supervisory authorities publish guidance and run registration portals. You also contact them to report a breach.

Getting started

How to start with minimal effort

You do not need a compliance department. These steps are ordered so the cheapest, highest-value work comes first — most organisations manage the first four in a week.

1

Find out which law applies

Look up the country where your customers, staff or users are. Note the regulator, the breach deadline, and whether you must register.

30 minutes
2

Write down what you collect

A single spreadsheet: what personal data you hold, why, where it lives, who can see it, and who you share it with.

An afternoon
3

Name someone accountable

Even where the law does not demand a DPO, pick a person. Privacy that belongs to everyone belongs to no one.

An hour
4

Publish a plain-language privacy notice

Say what you collect, why, who you share it with, how long you keep it, and what rights people have. Write it for a customer, not a court.

A day
5

Delete what you should not have

Old exports, abandoned spreadsheets, shared logins. Reduce who can reach personal data, and set retention periods.

A week
6

Agree what happens if there is a breach

One page: who is told first, who decides whether it is reportable, who contacts the regulator, and the deadline.

Half a day
7

Then, and only then, scale up

Vendor agreements, transfer mechanisms, DPIAs for high-risk projects, registration and audits where required.
