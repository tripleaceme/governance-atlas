What data security is really about

Personal data protection is mostly about permission: whether you are allowed to hold information about someone and what you owe them in return. Data security is about custody: whether the data you hold stays where you put it, unchanged, and readable only by the people who should see it.

The two are joined at the hip. Every privacy law in the world contains a clause requiring "appropriate technical and organisational measures" to keep personal data safe, and almost none of them says what those measures are. That gap is what this page fills. The law names the duty; practice supplies the answer.

What surprises people is how ordinary the answer turns out to be. The incidents that actually cause harm are rarely sophisticated. They are a former employee whose account was never switched off, a database left open to the internet, a backup nobody had ever tried to restore, a supplier that got breached and carried the damage inward, or a convincing email on a busy afternoon. The controls that prevent them are unglamorous and largely the same in every country and every industry.

That is the useful thing about security: it does not vary the way regulation does. A strong password policy in Lagos is a strong password policy in Lisbon. Once you know the handful of controls that carry most of the risk, you can apply them anywhere and then check what your own regulator additionally demands on top.

Where data security sits

Three things get called "data security" and they behave very differently. Telling them apart saves a lot of confusion.

Regulation · varies by country

The security duty inside privacy law

Nearly every data protection law requires you to secure personal data and to report it when you fail. This part is country-specific, and it is legally binding: the deadline for reporting a breach and the size of the fine are set nationally.

Practice · same everywhere

Data security itself

The controls: who can log in, what they can reach, whether it is encrypted, whether there is a backup, whether anyone would notice an intrusion. These are engineering and management decisions, not legal ones, and they travel across borders unchanged.

Regulation · sector by sector

Cybersecurity regulation

National cyber acts, critical-infrastructure duties, and rules from banking, telecoms and health regulators. These usually apply to particular sectors rather than to everyone, and they overlap heavily with the privacy duty above.

This is why the Atlas describes data security rather than mapping it country by country. In most countries there is no single "data security law" to point at. The duties are scattered across a cybersecurity act, a handful of sector regulators, and one article of the privacy law — and where the boundary falls is a matter of interpretation, not record. Publishing a national verdict on it would mean inventing a distinction the source material does not make. The parts that are country-specific and enforceable — the security obligation and the breach deadline — are recorded on each country's page under personal data, where they legally live.

The words you will hear

The vocabulary of security conversations, audits, vendor questionnaires and incident calls.

Encryption at rest
Data stored on a disk, database or backup is scrambled, so a stolen laptop or copied database file is useless without the key.
Encryption in transit
Data is scrambled while moving across a network. This is what HTTPS does. It protects against interception, not against whoever receives it.
Key management
How encryption keys are created, stored, rotated and revoked. Encryption with the key left beside the data protects nothing.
Multi-factor authentication (MFA)
Requiring something beyond a password — a code, an app prompt, a hardware key. The single highest-value control most organisations are missing.
Least privilege
Each person and system gets the minimum access needed to do the job, and no more. It limits how far an intruder can travel once inside.
Role-based access control (RBAC)
Granting access by job role rather than to individuals, so joiners, movers and leavers are handled by changing one role.
Access review
Periodically checking who still has access to what, and removing what is no longer needed. Dormant accounts are a recurring cause of breaches.
Data classification
Labelling data by how sensitive it is, so the strongest controls go where the greatest harm would be. Not everything needs the same protection.
Patching
Applying vendor updates that fix known flaws. Most successful intrusions exploit a weakness that had a fix available.
Vulnerability management
Continuously finding weaknesses across your systems, ranking them by risk, and tracking them to closure — rather than patching whatever is loudest.
Immutable backup
A backup copy that cannot be altered or deleted for a set period. This is what survives ransomware, because ordinary backups get encrypted too.
Logging and audit trail
A durable record of who did what and when. Without it you cannot tell whether an incident happened, how far it went, or what to report.
Incident response
The agreed plan for what happens when something goes wrong: who is called, who decides, who talks to the regulator, and in what order.
CERT / CSIRT
A Computer Emergency Response Team — the national or sector body that coordinates incident handling. Many countries require serious incidents to be reported to theirs.
Phishing
Tricking a person into giving up credentials or approving access. Still the most common way an intruder gets their first foothold.
Ransomware
Malware that encrypts your data and demands payment. It is simultaneously a security incident, an availability failure, and usually a reportable personal data breach.
Penetration test
A controlled, authorised attack on your own systems to find what a real attacker would. Useful once the basics are in place, wasteful before.
Pseudonymisation
Replacing identifying fields with tokens, so a leaked dataset is far less damaging. Recognised in several laws as a mitigating measure.

The practices that carry the most risk

Roughly ordered by how often their absence turns into a real incident. The first four prevent more harm than everything after them combined.

Highest impact

Know what you hold and where it lives

You cannot protect data you have forgotten about, and forgotten data is where breaches happen: the old export on a shared drive, the test database with real records, the retired system nobody switched off. Inventory first, then classify by sensitivity.

Highest impact

Control who can get in

Unique named accounts for everyone, multi-factor authentication everywhere it is offered, and same-day removal when someone leaves. No shared logins, no shared admin password, no accounts that outlive the person.

Highest impact

Give the least access that works

Most people do not need to see everything, and most systems do not need full database rights. Grant by role, review periodically, and make broad access a deliberate exception rather than the default.

Highest impact

Back up — and prove you can restore

Keep more than one copy, keep at least one somewhere separate, and keep one that cannot be altered or deleted. Then actually test a restore. An untested backup is a belief, not a control.

High

Encrypt in transit and at rest

HTTPS everywhere, disk and database encryption on, and keys stored somewhere other than beside the data. This is what turns a stolen device or a copied file into a non-event.

High

Patch and update on a schedule

Turn on automatic updates where you can, and keep a list of what you run so you know when something you depend on is affected. Attackers overwhelmingly use flaws that already have a fix.

High

Manage the people you hand data to

Your cloud host, payroll firm, analytics tool and agency all hold your data, and their breach becomes your breach. Know who they are, ask what they do, and put it in the contract.

Medium

Keep logs you could actually read

Record access and changes, keep the records long enough to be useful, and store them where an intruder cannot quietly edit them. Logs are how you answer "what was taken?" — the question every regulator asks first.

Medium

Train people against the realistic attack

Not an annual slideshow. Short, specific guidance on the things that actually happen: unexpected payment requests, urgent messages from the boss, login prompts that arrive at odd moments, and how to report a mistake without being punished for it.

Medium

Have an incident plan you have rehearsed

One page is enough: who is called, who decides, who contacts the regulator, and what the deadline is. Write it before you need it — the clock on breach notification starts when you become aware, not when you finish investigating.

Who you will be talking to

Security is not a solo activity, and most of it is not a purchase. These are the people who make it work.

From day one

Whoever runs your systems

Your engineers, IT provider or managed service partner already hold most of the answers — what is running, who can reach it, what is backed up. Almost every practice on this page is implemented by them.

From day one

An accountable owner

One named person answerable for security decisions. It does not need to be a specialist, but it does need to be someone who can make a call and be asked about it later.

Early

Your privacy or data protection lead

Breach reporting sits at the seam between security and privacy. Whoever handles your data protection obligations must be in the incident plan, because the legal clock is theirs to watch.

When you depend on them

Your critical vendors

The suppliers who host, process or transmit your data. Ask what they do, ask what they will tell you and how fast if they are breached, and get it written down.

Once the basics hold

An external tester or assessor

A penetration tester or auditor finds what you cannot see yourself. Valuable after the fundamentals are in place — before that, they will simply hand you a list you already know.

Before the incident

Your national CERT or sector regulator

Find out now whether your country or sector requires incident reporting, to whom, and within how long. Discovering this during an incident is the worst possible time.

How to start with minimal effort

No budget required for most of this. The order matters more than the speed — each step makes the next one cheaper.

1An afternoon

List every place data lives

Systems, cloud services, spreadsheets, laptops, phones, backups, and anything a supplier holds for you. The list will be incomplete and that is fine — an incomplete list still beats an assumption.

2A day

Turn on MFA and clean out old accounts

Enable multi-factor authentication on email, cloud, banking and admin tools first. Then list every account and remove the ones belonging to people who have left. This is the single highest-return day of work available to you.

3A day

Check what a normal account can reach

Log in as an ordinary user and see how much of the data you can open. Whatever surprises you is where to reduce access first.

4Half a day

Test restoring one backup

Pick a real system and restore it somewhere safe. You are checking two things: that the backup works, and how long it takes. Both are answers you need before an incident, not during.

5An hour

Switch on encryption and automatic updates

Device encryption, database encryption and auto-update are usually settings you already own and have not enabled. Turn them on before considering anything you have to buy.

6An hour

Write the one-page incident plan

Who is called, who decides, who notifies the regulator, and by when. Check your country's breach deadline on its country page and put the actual number in the plan.

7Ongoing

Then, and only then, scale up

Formal frameworks, certification, monitoring tooling and penetration testing all pay off — but only once the six steps above hold. Buying them first is how organisations end up certified and still breached.

Further reading

Established, freely available frameworks. They overlap heavily and differ mainly in depth — start with the shortest.

CIS Critical Security Controls ↗
A prioritised, plainly written list of controls with a defined starting tier for small organisations. The most practical place to begin.
NIST Cybersecurity Framework ↗
Organises security into Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover. Widely used as a common vocabulary between technical and non-technical stakeholders.
ISO/IEC 27001 ↗
The international standard for an information security management system, and the certification most often demanded in enterprise contracts and tenders.
OWASP Top 10 ↗
The most common weaknesses in web applications. Essential if you build software; largely irrelevant if you only buy it.
NCSC small business guidance ↗
Short, free, and written for organisations without a security team. Useful regardless of where you operate.
ENISA guidance ↗
The EU cybersecurity agency's material on risk management and incident handling, including guidance aimed at smaller organisations.
A note on frameworks. None of these is a law, and adopting one does not by itself make you compliant with any country's regulation. They are useful because regulators, auditors and enterprise customers recognise them — and because they save you from designing a security programme from first principles.
