What "general data" actually covers

Personal data protection has a clear centre: information about identifiable people. General data regulation does not. It is the collection of rules that treat data as an asset rather than as something belonging to a person — where it must physically live, what may cross a border, who may reuse public datasets, what obligations attach to industrial and machine- generated data, and what the newer AI and data acts require.

Those rules are real, but they are patchy. A country may have a strict data localisation requirement and nothing else. Another may have an open data regime and an AI act but no position on non-personal data at all. There is rarely a single instrument to point at, and the instruments that exist are named differently everywhere.

That makes the field hard to describe by listing regulations. It is much easier to describe by the work it governs — which is where an established framework helps.

The DAMA framework as a map of the field

DAMA International's Data Management Body of Knowledge (DMBOK) organises data management into eleven areas around a governance core. It is not law, and no country legislates it — but it is the most widely recognised map of what managing data actually involves, and it shows where regulation lands and where it does not.

The core

Data governance

Who decides, who is accountable, and how policy is set and enforced. Every other area depends on it. Increasingly touched by regulation through accountability and record-keeping duties.

Heavily regulated

Data security

Protecting data from unauthorised access and loss. Regulated through privacy law, cybersecurity acts and sector rules — and described as practice in the data security guide.

Heavily regulated

Data storage and operations

Where data physically lives and how it is run day to day. This is the area data localisation and residency requirements act on directly, and it is the most common form of general data regulation worldwide.

Partly regulated

Data integration and interoperability

Moving and combining data between systems and organisations. Touched by cross-border transfer rules, data sharing and portability mandates, and the newer data-access acts.

Partly regulated

Document and content management

Unstructured records and their lifecycle. Where statutory retention periods, records management rules and freedom-of-information regimes apply.

Partly regulated

Reference and master data

The authoritative version of core entities — customers, products, locations. Regulated where national identity systems, registries or mandated code lists are involved.

Partly regulated

Data quality

Whether data is accurate, complete and fit for use. Most privacy regulations contain an accuracy principle, and sector regulators often set reporting-quality standards.

Partly regulated

Metadata

Data about your data: definitions, lineage, ownership. Rarely regulated by name, but it is what makes records of processing, retention schedules and audit responses possible at all.

Mostly practice

Data architecture

How data assets are structured across the organisation and how that structure serves the business. Regulated only indirectly, through requirements it has to satisfy.

Mostly practice

Data modelling and design

How data is represented and organised. An engineering discipline; regulation cares about its outcomes rather than its methods.

Mostly practice

Warehousing and business intelligence

Consolidating data for analysis and reporting. Where regulation bites, it is usually through purpose limitation and the reuse of personal data for analytics.

Emerging

AI and automated decisions

Not a DMBOK area, but the fastest-moving edge of general data regulation. AI acts, automated-decision rules and training-data provisions increasingly sit alongside the framework rather than inside it.

Why this framework and not a national one. DMBOK is vendor-neutral, widely taught, and already the shared vocabulary in most data teams — so it lets this area be described once rather than eleven times. Where countries actually differ

These are the questions the country pages answer. Everything else in the framework above is practice you can apply anywhere.

✓

Data localisation: must certain data be stored on servers physically inside the country? Common for government, health, financial and telecoms data.

✓

Cross-border restrictions: what may leave, under what conditions, and does anything require prior approval?

✓

Non-personal and industrial data: are there rules on machine-generated, IoT or industrial data, and on who may access or reuse it?

✓

Open data: is there a statutory regime for publishing and reusing public-sector data?

✓

AI and data acts: is there an AI act, a data act, or a data governance act in force or in progress?
