The short version

Compiled from public sources · not legal advice

Estonia is governed directly by the GDPR, supplemented by the Personal Data Protection Act (isikuandmete kaitse seadus), adopted in December 2018 and in force since 15 January 2019. The Act covers the areas the GDPR leaves to member states — law-enforcement processing, journalistic and research exemptions, deceased persons' data — and sets the digital age of consent at 13. It also defines the mandate of the supervisory authority, the Estonian Data Protection Inspectorate (Andmekaitse Inspektsioon, AKI) in Tallinn.

Estonia's distinctive wrinkle is procedural: it has no general administrative-fine mechanism, so GDPR penalties of up to €20 million or 4% of worldwide turnover are imposed through the national misdemeanour procedure. This long limited practical enforcement, and the Penal Code was amended on 1 November 2023 specifically to make the higher fine ceilings workable. AKI is nonetheless active through precepts, orders and guidance, in a country whose famously digital government (X-Road, e-residency) makes data governance a high-profile national topic.

For organisations, compliance is standard GDPR practice — lawful basis, transparency, 72-hour breach notification, DPIAs and Chapter V transfer safeguards — with the local nuance that formal fining runs through misdemeanour proceedings rather than pure administrative decisions.

What you actually have to do

  • 01
    Comply with full GDPR obligations — Lawful basis, transparency, data subject rights, records of processing, DPIAs and accountability apply directly under the GDPR as supplemented by the Personal Data Protection Act.
  • 02
    Notify breaches within 72 hours — Personal data breaches posing a risk to individuals must be reported to the Data Protection Inspectorate within 72 hours.
  • 03
    Appoint a DPO where required — Mandatory for public authorities and organisations with large-scale monitoring or special-category processing as core activities.
  • 04
    Obtain parental consent under 13 — Under section 8 of the Act, online services relying on consent may accept consent only from children aged 13 or above; younger children need parental authorisation.
  • 05
    Safeguard cross-border transfers — Transfers outside the EU/EEA require adequacy, standard contractual clauses or other GDPR Chapter V safeguards.
