The short version

Compiled from public sources · not legal advice

Indonesia's Law No. 27 of 2022 on Personal Data Protection (UU PDP) is Southeast Asia's largest GDPR-style framework, consolidating more than 30 fragmented sectoral rules into one law that covers both digital and physical processing by public and private controllers, with extraterritorial reach where processing has legal effects in Indonesia. Its two-year transition period ended on 17 October 2024, so obligations are fully binding.

The law's centerpiece obligations are familiar: lawful bases with explicit consent as the default, data subject rights, mandatory breach notification within 3×24 hours, DPO appointment above thresholds, and cross-border transfers conditioned on equivalent protection or safeguards. Administrative fines can reach 2% of annual revenue, and the law also carries criminal offences for unlawfully obtaining, disclosing, or falsifying personal data — at least 23 criminal cases have already invoked it.

The main gap is institutional: the independent supervisory agency (Lembaga PDP) the law mandates has still not been established. A draft Presidential Regulation was published in February 2026 and awaits presidential approval, with an operational target during 2026. In the meantime, enforcement sits with Komdigi's Directorate General of Digital Space Supervision, and the key implementing government regulation is still being finalized — so administrative fining practice remains untested.

What you actually have to do

  • 01
    Establish a lawful basis — Process personal data only on a valid basis — explicit, recorded consent by default, or contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task, or legitimate interests.
  • 02
    Report breaches within 3×24 hours — Notify the regulator and affected data subjects in writing within 72 hours of a failure of personal data protection, describing the data involved and remediation.
  • 03
    Appoint a DPO where required — Designate a data protection officer if processing is for public services, involves large-scale regular monitoring, or large-scale sensitive/specific data.
  • 04
    Safeguard cross-border transfers — Verify the destination country offers equivalent protection, or put adequate binding safeguards in place, or obtain the data subject's consent before transferring data abroad.
  • 05
    Honor data subject rights — Provide access, correction, deletion, portability, objection, and consent-withdrawal mechanisms within statutory timelines.
