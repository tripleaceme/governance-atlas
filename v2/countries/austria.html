The short version

Compiled from public sources · not legal advice

Austria applies the GDPR directly, supplemented by the Datenschutzgesetz (DSG), whose Section 1 uniquely enshrines data protection as a constitutional right with direct third-party effect — a feature that predates and survives the GDPR. The DSG fills member-state discretion areas: it lowers the digital consent age to 14, sets detailed rules on image processing and video surveillance, protects DPO confidentiality, and adds criminal penalties for intentional data misuse.

The supervisory authority is the Datenschutzbehörde (DSB) in Vienna, which handles a heavy complaint caseload — Austria's active privacy litigation culture (home of noyb and Max Schrems) keeps it busy. Austrian enforcement has produced significant fines, including EUR 16 million against Austrian Post for profiling political affinity data and EUR 8 million against Rewe International, and Austrian complaints have driven landmark EU-level rulings on tracking and international transfers.

Practical compliance is standard GDPR — lawful basis, transparency, 72-hour breach notification, DPIAs, DPOs where triggered — with Austrian specifics around video surveillance, employee data (works council involvement), and the age-14 rule for information-society services.

What you actually have to do

  • 01
    Lawful basis and transparency — All processing needs a GDPR Art. 6 basis with clear privacy information; the constitutional right in DSG §1 also binds private actors.
  • 02
    Notify breaches within 72 hours — Report personal data breaches to the DSB within 72 hours; inform affected individuals when risk is high.
  • 03
    Follow video surveillance rules — The DSG imposes specific labelling, logging and proportionality requirements on image processing and CCTV.
  • 04
    Appoint a DPO where required — Public bodies and organisations with large-scale monitoring or sensitive-data processing must designate a DPO, who enjoys statutory confidentiality protection.
  • 05
    Respect the age-14 consent rule — Consent-based information-society services offered to children are valid from age 14; younger children need parental authorisation.
