The short version

Compiled from public sources · not legal advice

Kiribati has moved decisively from having no data protection framework to actively legislating one. The Data Protection Bill 2025 — a first for the country — passed its first reading unanimously in the Maneaba ni Maungatabu (Parliament) on 1 April 2025 and was expected to proceed to a second reading in a subsequent session. The Bill would be cited as the Data Protection Act 2025 and commences on a date the Minister appoints by notice; final passage and commencement could not be verified from official sources as of August 2026, so it is recorded here as a bill in progress.

The Bill is a modern, GDPR-influenced but deliberately lightweight framework tailored to a small island state. It applies to processing within Kiribati and extraterritorially where goods/services are offered to, or behaviour monitored of, people in Kiribati. Enforcement sits with the existing Digital Transformation Office (established under the Digital Government Act 2023) rather than a new standalone regulator, and the Office can investigate complaints, issue compliance notices, and impose administrative penalties up to AUD 20,000 (Kiribati uses the Australian dollar). A distinctive feature is the phased application: most domestic entities that are not 'controllers of major importance' (10,000+ data subjects) are exempt until the second anniversary of commencement, giving small local businesses a long runway.

Until the Bill is enacted and commenced, practical protections rest on the Cybercrime Act 2021 (confidentiality and computer-misuse offences) and sectoral confidentiality rules. Professionals should watch for the second-reading vote and the ministerial commencement notice, and note the two-year grace period for smaller controllers once in force.

What you actually have to do

  • 01
    Lawful and fair processing (proposed) — Controllers must process personal data fairly and transparently and only on a lawful basis under section 11 — consent, voluntary provision, contract, legal obligation, legal claims, public interest, medical or public-health emergency, legitimate interests, archiving/research, or data made public by the data subject.
  • 02
    Security of personal data (proposed) — Anyone processing personal data must implement appropriate technical and organisational measures (encryption, de-identification, resilience, regular testing) proportionate to the volume of data and likelihood of harm (section 18).
  • 03
    Breach records and notification (proposed) — All personal data breaches must be recorded and analysed; breaches likely to cause significant harm must be notified to the Office and each affected data subject as soon as practicable, with public notification permitted where direct contact is disproportionate (sections 19–20).
  • 04
    DPIAs for major controllers (proposed) — Controllers of major importance must carry out and submit a data protection impact assessment to the Office before high-risk processing affecting 10,000+ data subjects, and may need Office approval to proceed (section 21).
  • 05
    Cross-border adequate protection (proposed) — Before processing or transferring personal data outside Kiribati, a person must take reasonable steps to verify adequate protection — via comparable foreign law, binding corporate rules, contract clauses, codes or certification — unless consent or another section 24 basis applies (section 23).
