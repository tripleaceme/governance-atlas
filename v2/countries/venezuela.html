The short version

Compiled from public sources · not legal advice

Venezuela has no comprehensive data protection law and no data protection authority. Privacy protection rests on scattered instruments: Article 28 of the 1999 Constitution guarantees habeas data — the right to access one's personal data held in public or private registries and to seek correction or destruction — alongside Article 60's general privacy right and the secrecy of communications. A 2011 Constitutional Chamber (Supreme Tribunal) decision articulated data-processing principles drawn from these provisions, but they have never been implemented in a general statute.

What exists is sectoral: the Law on Data Messages and Electronic Signatures (2001), banking-sector confidentiality rules under the Law on Banking Sector Institutions (2014), the Infogobierno framework for state IT, and criminal provisions in the Special Law Against Computer Crimes. There is no DPO requirement, no registration, no breach notification duty, no administrative fines regime and no regulator to complain to. Major incidents — including the Movistar Venezuela breach in 2025 and the Cashea app breach in 2026 — have highlighted how little recourse affected individuals have beyond constitutional actions before courts of uneven independence.

For a meeting: assume no local compliance framework, but also high country risk around data handling — state surveillance concerns, weak rule of law, and the possibility that foreign regimes (GDPR, etc.) supply the applicable rules for any Venezuela-related processing. No serious draft data protection bill was identified as advancing in the National Assembly.
