The short version

Compiled from public sources · not legal advice

Tonga moved from having no data protection law at all to a comprehensive, GDPR-style regime with the Privacy Act 2025 (Act 34 of 2025), which received Royal Assent from King Tupou VI on 16 December 2025 and was published in the Government Gazette in early January 2026 alongside a companion Personal Health Information Protection Act 2025. The Act protects 'personal information' with definitions closely modelled on the GDPR (including sensitive categories such as biometric and genetic data), applies to data controllers and processors established in Tonga or processing within the Kingdom, and reaches offshore entities that target goods or services at, or monitor, people in Tonga. It binds the State, though public authorities enjoy broad carve-outs for national security, law enforcement and public-safety research, and there are exemptions for personal/household use and journalism.

The Act creates an independent Privacy Commission — a body corporate run by a single Privacy Commissioner chosen through an open, merit-based Appointments Committee process — with powers to investigate complaints, issue enforcement notices and information notices, accept enforceable undertakings, designate countries or contractual clauses as adequate for cross-border transfers, and enter and search premises. Controllers must have a lawful basis for processing (consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task or legitimate interests), give detailed collection notices, minimise and secure data, limit retention, and put written agreements in place with processors. Data subjects get rights of access, correction, deletion, direct-marketing opt-out, objection, consent withdrawal and protection against solely automated decisions; portability can be added by regulation. Enforcement is civil-court based rather than administrative: the Commission or Attorney-General sues for pecuniary penalties capped at TOP 100,000 for repeat corporate contraventions.

Enforcement reality is the key caveat for any meeting: the Act commences 'on a date proclaimed by Cabinet' (s.1(2)), and while local reporting treats the gazetted 2025 Acts as now law, no Cabinet commencement proclamation, Commissioner appointment or functioning Commission could be independently verified as of August 2026. The 72-hour breach-notification duty is expressly deferred until two years after commencement, and registration, impact assessments and portability all await ministerial regulations. Practically, organisations handling Tongan personal data should treat the Act as the operative standard and prepare now, but should not expect active supervision in the immediate term. Tonga holds no EU adequacy decision.

What you actually have to do

  • 01
    Establish a lawful basis for processing — Controllers may only process personal information with the data subject's unwithdrawn consent or where necessary for a contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public-interest task, legitimate interests, archiving/research or compliance with Tongan law (s.27); sensitive personal information requires a s.27 basis plus a further s.28 condition, and the controller bears the burden of proving consent.
  • 02
    Secure personal information — Every controller and processor must ensure the security, integrity and confidentiality of personal information through appropriate technical and organisational measures — risk identification, safeguards, verification and updating, with pseudonymisation and encryption cited as options (s.36).
  • 03
    Notify breaches within 72 hours (once operative) — Controllers must notify the Privacy Commission within 72 hours of becoming aware of a breach likely to risk individuals' rights and freedoms, communicate high-risk breaches to affected data subjects without undue delay, and keep records of all breaches — though s.37 does not apply until the second anniversary of the Act's commencement (s.37(11)).
  • 04
    Restrict cross-border transfers — Personal information may not be transferred outside the Kingdom unless the Commission gives written consent or the recipient is subject to a law, binding corporate rules, contractual clauses, code of conduct or certification affording adequate protection, with derogations for informed consent and contractual necessity (ss.39-40).
  • 05
    Honour data subject rights and retention limits — Controllers must provide free access, copies, correction and deletion (s.42), stop direct-marketing processing within 30 days of a request (s.43), respect objection, consent-withdrawal and automated-decision rights (ss.44-46), give detailed collection notices (s.33), and retain personal information no longer than necessary, then return, destroy or permanently de-identify it (s.34).
