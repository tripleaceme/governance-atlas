The short version

Compiled from public sources · not legal advice

Chile enacted Law No. 21.719 in 2024 (published in the Diario Oficial on 13 December 2024), a full GDPR-style replacement of its pioneering but toothless 1999 statute, Law 19.628. The catch for anyone doing business in Chile: the new law applies from 1 December 2026, so as of August 2026 the old Law 19.628 — consent-based, with no supervisory authority and no meaningful fines — remains the operative regime while companies race to prepare.

Law 21.719 creates Chile's first data protection authority, the Agencia de Protección de Datos Personales, an autonomous public body that begins functions in December 2026. It introduces legal bases beyond consent (including legitimate interest), data subject rights including portability, breach reporting, extraterritorial scope, cross-border transfer rules, and fines of up to 20,000 UTM for very serious infringements — a dramatic escalation from the current regime. A distinctive feature is the voluntary 'infringement prevention model' (compliance program) that can be certified and mitigates sanctions.

Enforcement reality today is minimal — claims under Law 19.628 run through the courts — but that changes on 1 December 2026 when the Agency's supervisory and sanctioning powers switch on. Chile does not hold an EU adequacy decision, though the new law was designed with adequacy ambitions in mind.

What you actually have to do

  • 01
    Prepare for December 2026 — Map processing to the new legal bases (consent, contract, legal obligation, legitimate interest) and remediate gaps before Law 21.719 applies on 1 December 2026.
  • 02
    Honor expanded data subject rights — Access, rectification, cancellation, opposition, and new rights such as portability must be operationalized with defined response procedures.
  • 03
    Secure data and report breaches — Implement appropriate security measures and be ready to report significant breaches to the new Agency without undue delay.
  • 04
    Consider a certified compliance model — Adopting the law's infringement-prevention model (including a designated data protection delegate) can substantially mitigate sanctions.
  • 05
    Put transfer safeguards in place — Cross-border transfers will require adequacy, appropriate guarantees, or a statutory exception under the new regime.
