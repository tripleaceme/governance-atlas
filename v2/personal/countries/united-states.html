Executive summary

Compiled from public sources · not legal advice

The United States still has no comprehensive federal privacy law. Protection comes from a patchwork: sectoral federal statutes (HIPAA, GLBA, COPPA, FCRA), FTC enforcement of unfair and deceptive practices under Section 5 of the FTC Act, and a fast-growing layer of state comprehensive privacy laws. As of mid-2026, 25 states have enacted comprehensive consumer privacy laws — about 20 in effect and five more (Oklahoma, Alabama, Louisiana, Georgia, Vermont) taking effect in 2027–2028 — most following the Virginia model of notice, consumer rights, opt-outs for sale/targeted advertising, and consent for sensitive data. California remains the outlier with its own dedicated regulator (CPPA) and rulemaking.

Federal legislation remains at the bill stage: the American Privacy Rights Act expired with the 118th Congress in January 2025, and new comprehensive proposals — the SECURE Data Act and GUARD Financial Data Act, introduced in April 2026 — are pending with uncertain prospects. Separately, the DOJ's bulk sensitive data rule (under Executive Order 14117) now restricts transfers of Americans' sensitive data to countries of concern, a national-security-driven form of export control rather than privacy law.

For EU transfers, the EU–US Data Privacy Framework adequacy decision (July 2023) remains in force: the EU General Court upheld it in Latombe v Commission in September 2025, though an appeal to the CJEU is pending. Enforcement reality is active but fragmented — FTC consent decrees, state AG actions, and California CPPA orders — so multistate businesses typically build to the strictest applicable state standard.

Key obligations

  • 01
    Map and honor state consumer rights — Provide access, deletion, correction, and portability rights and honor opt-outs of sale, targeted advertising, and profiling in the 20+ states with effective comprehensive laws, including universal opt-out signals (e.g., GPC) where required.
  • 02
    Obtain consent for sensitive data — Most state laws require opt-in consent (or, in California, use-limitation rights) for sensitive data such as health, biometrics, precise geolocation, and children's data; separate health-data laws (e.g., Washington My Health My Data) add private rights of action.
  • 03
    Notify breaches under state law — Comply with all 50 states' breach notification statutes — timelines and thresholds vary — plus sectoral rules (HIPAA, GLBA, SEC disclosure).
  • 04
    Conduct data protection assessments — Most Virginia-model states require documented assessments for targeted advertising, sale, profiling, and sensitive-data processing.
  • 05
    Comply with COPPA and teen protections — Obtain verifiable parental consent before collecting personal information from children under 13, and track expanding state protections for teens.
